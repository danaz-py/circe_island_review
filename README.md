# circe_island_review

This is a school project for my sophomore year in 2026. Based on the text, the Odyssey, I created a spin off review website on Aeaea and named it Circe's Island Resort. This project was entirely made by me with MINIMAL use of AI.

The Odyssey Creative Project Artist Statement
The rise of social media allowed for the development of widespread psychological persuasion. However, human manipulation has always existed, way before things like Instagram were ever conceived. Since the beginning of time, people have told stories of tricksters and the tricked, stories of characters like Odysseus and the suitors. That is why I chose the subject of human manipulation, specifically in the realm of social media—because I want to shed light on the naturalness of duplicity in humans. 

The individuals that we find in Robert Fagles’s translation of The Odyssey are not much different from ourselves. We like satisfied stomachs, hospitality (xenia), and glory (kleos). We have superstitions; black cats will still strike fear in people while others will laugh, just as eagles sent from Zeus will strike fear in bird prophets while others will jeer. We succumb to gratification, whether it is the desire for gifts or one more scroll. Most of all, to get that gratification, we manipulate. 

For all sorts of reasons, humans lie to themselves and to each other. Perhaps I will convince myself that I am not a hypocrite when I smack my lips while eating, even when I get mad when others do. Or perhaps a goddess will convince herself that keeping a man who longs for home captive on her island is out of love and for his own good. In 1954, psychologist Leon Festinger and colleagues studied this phenomenon of self-deception in When the Prophecy Fails. They infiltrated a small cult led by a woman named Marion Keech, who claimed that an apocalyptic flood would occur on December 21, 1954. When no flood occurred, the group faced extreme cognitive dissonance; they had given up their job and family for this prophecy, only to find out the prophecy was entirely wrong. However, instead of admitting that they were wrong, the cult members resolved the dissonance by creating a new narrative: their faith was so strong that God had decided to spare the world. This exact same mechanism drives modern internet behavior—online communities will build deep loyalties around influencers or trends, and when these influencers face public backlash or trends are deemed to be offensive or unfunny, people will attempt to protect their ego and kleos by insisting their actions were misunderstood or taken out of context. 

Just like the cult members in When the Prophecy Fails, Odysseus convinces himself and his men that remaining on Aeaea is a rational and necessary opportunity for rest. Upon arrival, Circe offers the men lavish feasts, dark red wine, and endless indulgence. This comfort is revealed to be a deceptive facade, and some of the men get turned into swine. But let’s face it, this is unsurprising, and it’s even more unsurprising when Odysseus gets his daily dose of godly help and breaks Circe’s spell, freeing his crew. However, what is truly jarring is when the famous Odysseus heeds Circe’s offer to rest on her island; he tells his crew, “‘Then hurry, all of you, come along with me / to see our friends in the magic halls of Circe, / eating and drinking—the feast flows on forever’” (Homer 10.467-471). Notice how Odysseus advertises Circe’s palace to his remaining crew. He frames it using hyperbolic language, “the feast flows on forever,” while omitting the trauma of being turned into swine and the danger of losing one’s identity in Circe’s halls. This moment echoes the exact mechanism of social media: selectively grasp the most enticing aspects, ignore unattractive details, and spread the word. Remarkably, Odysseus and his posse remain on the island for a full year, lulled into complacency by endless food, wine, and leisure: “day in, day out, till a year had run its course, / feasting on sides of meat and drafts of heady wine” (Homer 10.515–516). Because these men are not strangers to alcohol, Odysseus remarking that the wine was “heady” is to imply something more potent; they were not just intoxicated from the alcohol, but from the luxurious environment. When one is drunk, critical thinking becomes arduous and susceptible to pleasant indulgences, removing senses of agency. This exactly mirrors the act of doomscrolling; it’s easier to keep scrolling than to face one’s responsibilities. Isn’t it bizarre how the ancient Greeks and modern society share the exact same habits?

But differences do exist between us and the characters in the Odyssey: we are much more educated about the consequences of impulsive pleasure. In the world of the Odyssey, characters can only rely on divine word and the stories of bards, one of which is very hard to obtain unless your name is Odysseus, and the other (most of the time) is limited to one mouth. However, if we don't know whether a product is worth spending our money on, we can check review sites and instant message to get feedback from hundreds of people. That is why I created a website for Circe’s Island Resort, a more modern twist on propaganda.

I coded a travel review website called TripHelper for a resort based on Aeaea called Circe’s Island Resort using Flutter and Dart. Flutter is a framework for building apps and websites, while Dart is the programming language used to write them. The website displays resort details (description, location, ratings, and image gallery) and allows users to submit reviews with ratings and feedback. This website is paired with my partner, Rachel, who created Instagram posts on Canva to advertise Circe’s Island Resort. Our pairing of projects is to model modern advertising: the consumer sees the product on social media, then logs onto a review website to confirm its authenticity. 

Though new to Flutter and Dart, my strong background in coding and passion for User Interface (UI) design made me excited rather than intimidated to test my skills on this project. With access to popular review sites such as TripAdvisor, developing the UI and the layout for the website was easy. However, adding depth to a review site required a lot more thinking. I knew I couldn’t just create a pretty copy of TripAdvisor and call it a day (even though it took extremely long just to do so). By adding features like Deleted Reviews and Circe’s responses to reviews, I transformed the site into a mockery of how people attempt to save face on the internet. This interactive dynamic directly mirrors how influencers and corporations manipulate users in an attempt to squeeze out more cash or simply protect their reputations.

While I am proud of the functional depth achieved in Flutter, the visual representation of the resort remains the least pleasing aspect of the project. Since Aeaea is, frankly, not real, I relied on generic internet images of contemporary beaches and meals. Consequently, the imagery makes the experience a lot less immersive than I would have liked.
It’s uncomfortable to acknowledge our intrinsic nature to manipulate each other and ourselves to get what we want. I still struggle to fully admit that, yes, I can be avoidant of tasks that serve my future self because I dislike having to actively resist easy-access pleasure like a YouTube video or grabbing a snack. I know it is true, but even as I type this, I still feel uncomfortable, as if I don’t truly believe in it. So while social media platforms have amplified the magnitude, the psychological mechanisms driving them—cognitive dissonance and ego/kleos protection—have always been the true drivers.
